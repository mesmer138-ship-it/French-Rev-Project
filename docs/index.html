The project

Can the charge, the class and the date explain who was executed?

Between 1792 and 1795 the Paris Revolutionary Tribunal tried thousands of people. This project asks how much of the outcome, execution or acquittal, can be predicted from three things: the wording of the charge, the defendant's social class and the date of the trial.

I trained a model on 5,264 charges recorded by the historian Wallon. Then I used it to read the accusations against six people drawn from the records of the Committee of Public Safety, and to show what it makes of each one.

5,264charges in the dataset
0.863AUC on 531 held-out trials
0.66AUC on trials later than training

Date and class do most of the work: alone they reach 0.81. The charge text adds a little. And the model is much weaker on trials later than the ones it was trained on, which is the result to keep in mind when reading any score on this site.

The sources

Three names to know.

Wallon

Henri-Alexandre Wallon was a French historian and statesman, later known as one of the founders of the Third Republic. His Histoire du tribunal révolutionnaire de Paris, avec le journal de ses actes (six volumes, 1880–1882) compiles the tribunal's case record: defendants, charges, outcomes and dates.

It is the source of every outcome in the dataset. It is a 19th-century compilation, not the raw archives, so some editorial handling sits between the original trials and the data.

The Committee of Public Safety

The Committee of Public Safety was created by the National Convention in April 1793 and became the working executive of the Terror, directing the war effort and the repression of suspected enemies of the Republic.

Its decisions and correspondence are published in Aulard's Recueil des actes du Comité de salut public. The accusations against the six people on this site are extracted from it.

Le Moniteur

Le Moniteur universel was a Paris daily newspaper founded in November 1789 that became the Revolution's de facto official gazette, printing the debates of the assemblies and government announcements.

Where the Committee's records are thin for one of the six people, Le Moniteur supplies additional dated passages.

    How to read the scores

    A score is not a verdict.

    It estimates how often similar past cases ended in execution. It does not measure guilt, and it is not a calibrated probability.

    Scores move with the wording of a charge: rewordings of the same accusation scored anywhere between 0.23 and 0.73. That is why each person gets a range, not one number.
