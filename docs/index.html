The project

Can the charge, the class and the date explain who was executed?

Between 1792 and 1795 the Paris Revolutionary Tribunal tried thousands of people. This project asks how much of the outcome, execution or acquittal, can be predicted from three things: the wording of the charge, the defendant's social class and the date of the trial.

The historian Wallon recorded 5,264 charges. After removing duplicates, 4,220 remained, and I trained a model on 3,484 of them. Then I used it to read the accusations against the people on this site, drawn from the records of the Committee of Public Safety and from Le Moniteur, and to show what it makes of each one.

0.86AUC on 736 held-out trials
0.75AUC on trials later than training

Date and class do most of the work: together they reach 0.81, and the charge text lifts that to 0.86. On trials later than the ones it was trained on, the date stops helping, and the model scores 0.75, almost all of it from the charge and the class.

The code, notebooks and extraction pipeline are on GitHub.

    The sources

    Three names to know.

    Wallon

    Henri-Alexandre Wallon was a French historian and statesman, later known as one of the founders of the Third Republic. His Histoire du tribunal révolutionnaire de Paris, avec le journal de ses actes (six volumes, 1880–1882) compiles the tribunal's case record: defendants, charges, outcomes and dates.

    It is the source of every outcome in the dataset. It is a 19th-century compilation, not the raw archives, so some editorial handling sits between the original trials and the data.

    The Committee of Public Safety

    The Committee of Public Safety was created by the National Convention in April 1793 and became the working executive of the Terror, directing the war effort and the repression of suspected enemies of the Republic.

    Its decisions and correspondence are published in Aulard's Recueil des actes du Comité de salut public (Collection of the Acts of the Committee of Public Safety). Many of the accusations on this site are extracted from it.

    Le Moniteur

    Le Moniteur universel was a Paris daily newspaper founded in November 1789 that became the Revolution's de facto official gazette, printing the debates of the assemblies and government announcements.

    Most of the debates, denunciations and trial reports on this site come from Le Moniteur: the Convention's sittings, the clubs, and the indictments read in court.

    Background

    From a king's trial to the Terror.

    In 1789 the Revolution turned France from an absolute monarchy into a constitutional one. Louis XVI kept his crown, but lost the trust of the Revolution when he tried to flee Paris with his family in June 1791 and was stopped at Varennes.

    In April 1792 France went to war with Austria, soon joined by Prussia. On 10 August 1792 crowds stormed the Tuileries palace; the monarchy was suspended and the royal family was locked in the Temple. A week later, on 17 August, a first special tribunal was set up in Paris to try the “crimes of 10 August”; it sat until the end of November 1792. A new assembly, the National Convention, abolished the monarchy and declared the Republic on 22 September 1792, the first day of Year I. That is also day zero for the dates in this project's model.

    The Convention tried the king itself. In January 1793 it found “Louis Capet” guilty of conspiring against liberty and, by a narrow majority, sentenced him to death. He was guillotined in Paris on 21 January 1793.

    The spring that followed was a crisis: war with Britain and more of Europe, a royalist rising in the Vendée, and in April the defection of General Dumouriez to the Austrians. The Convention answered with new institutions. On 10 March 1793 it created a new, permanent court, the Revolutionary Tribunal, to try crimes against the Republic, with no appeal. Wallon's record, and this project's data, cover both courts. On 6 April it created the Committee of Public Safety, which became the working government of the Terror.

    The Law of Suspects (September 1793) let the authorities arrest anyone whose conduct or connections looked hostile, and the law of 22 Prairial (June 1794) sped up trials and stripped away most of the defence. Executions in Paris peaked that summer. On 9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794) the Convention turned on Robespierre, and the Terror wound down. The Tribunal was abolished in May 1795.
